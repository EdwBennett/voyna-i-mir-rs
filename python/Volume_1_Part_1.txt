
I
Thus spoke in July 1805 the well-known Anna Pavlovna Scherer, maid of honor and confidante of Empress Maria Feodorovna, as she greeted the important and high-ranking Prince Vasily, the first to arrive at her evening gathering.

II
The highest society of Petersburg began to arrive: people most varied in age and character, yet alike in the society in which they all lived; Prince Vasily’s daughter, the beautiful Hélène, also arrived to collect her father so that they could go together to the ambassador’s reception.

III
Apart from the aunt, beside whom sat only one elderly lady with a thin, tear-stained face, somewhat out of place in that brilliant society, the company divided into three groups.

IV
Although Prince Vasily listened to the elderly lady reluctantly and almost discourteously, even showing impatience, she smiled at him tenderly and touchingly and, so that he would not leave, took his hand.

V
Stout, taller than average, broad, with huge red hands, he did not know, as the saying goes, how to enter a drawing room, and knew even less how to leave one—that is, how to say something especially pleasant before departing.

VI
In the middle of supper Prince Andrei leaned on his elbow and, like a man who has long had something on his mind and suddenly resolves to speak out, began to speak with an expression of nervous irritation in which Pierre had never before seen his friend.

VII
The Emperor was informed about him, and unlike others he was transferred to the Semyonovsky Guards Regiment as an ensign.

VIII
The countess looked at her guest with a pleasant smile, though not concealing that she would not be at all upset if the visitor now rose and left.

IX
Sonya was a slim, petite brunette, with a soft gaze shaded by long eyelashes, a thick black braid wound twice around her head, and a yellowish hue to the skin of her face and especially of her bare, slender yet graceful and muscular arms and neck.

X
Natasha, now quiet, peered from her hiding place, waiting to see what he would do.

XI
The countess wanted to speak privately with her childhood friend, Princess Anna Mikhaylovna, whom she had not properly seen since her arrival from Petersburg.

XII
Although someone’s carriage stood at the entrance, the porter looked over the mother and son—who, without asking to be announced, had gone directly into the glass vestibule between two rows of statues in niches—and, after giving the old cloak a significant look, asked whether they wished to see the princesses or the count.

XIII
When Boris entered, Pierre was pacing his room, occasionally stopping in corners, making threatening gestures toward the wall as though piercing an invisible enemy with a sword, looking sternly over his spectacles, and then beginning his walk again.

XIV
When Anna Mikhaylovna left with her son for Count Kirill Vladimirovich Bezukhov’s, Countess Rostova sat alone for a long time, pressing a handkerchief to her eyes.

XV
The count led the male guests to his study, offering them his hunting collection of Turkish pipes.

XVI
The colonel said that the manifesto declaring war had already been issued in Petersburg and that a copy he himself had seen had that day been delivered by courier to the commander in chief.

XVII
The count, spreading his cards like a fan, struggled against his habitual after-dinner sleepiness and laughed at everything.

XVIII
While at the Rostovs’ the sixth English country dance was being danced in the hall to the sound of musicians playing out of tune from fatigue, and weary servants and cooks prepared supper, Count Bezukhov suffered his sixth stroke.

XIX
While such conversations were taking place in the reception room and in the princess’s rooms, the carriage carrying Pierre, for whom someone had been sent, and Anna Mikhaylovna, who had considered it necessary to go with him, entered Count Bezukhov’s courtyard.

XX
The part of the room beyond the columns, where on one side stood a tall mahogany bed under silk curtains and on the other an enormous icon case, was brightly lit in red, as churches are lit during an evening service.

XXI
There was no one left in the reception room except Prince Vasily and the eldest princess, who sat beneath Catherine’s portrait and spoke animatedly about something.

XXII
At Bald Hills, the estate of Prince Nikolai Andreyevich Bolkonsky, they expected each day the arrival of young Prince Andrei with his wife; but the expectation did not disturb the orderly routine by which life proceeded in the old prince’s house.

XXIII
At that moment a carriage and a chaise drew up at the entrance, and Prince Andrei got out of the carriage, helped his little wife down, and let her go ahead.

XXIV
At the appointed hour, powdered and shaved, the prince came out to the dining room, where his daughter-in-law, Princess Marya, Mademoiselle Bourienne, and the prince’s architect—admitted to the table by one of the prince’s strange whims—were waiting for him.

XXV
At moments of departure and change in life, people capable of reflecting on their actions are usually seized by a serious frame of mind.
